# Investment recommendation

**We recommend a portfolio between the value-weighted portfolio and the original estimated tangency portfolio: use value weights as a benchmark, retain moderate active allocations, and impose limits on individual-stock concentration, leverage, and short selling.** The Stage 1–3 findings and the 2026 tracking results indicate that optimization has potential, but do not justify investing entirely in the unconstrained tangency portfolio.

First, Stage 1 demonstrates both the tangency portfolio's in-sample advantage and its estimation risk. Its in-sample annualized Sharpe ratio is **2.197**, compared with **1.269** for the value-weighted portfolio. However, these weights were selected to maximize the Sharpe ratio using the same historical observations, so this advantage cannot be treated as a guarantee of future performance. The original tangency portfolio has gross exposure of **5.389 times** net asset value. When the estimation window is shortened from 108 to 84 months, gross exposure rises to **8.812 times**. This sensitivity to the sample window shows how estimation errors can translate into extreme positions. [Stage 1 results](https://github.com/shimarinshi/fe5108-portfolio-theory-lab/blob/main/artifacts/stage1/portfolio_diagnostics.csv)

Second, the 2026 tracking results support retaining active allocations, but also show why cumulative return alone is insufficient for portfolio selection.

| Portfolio | Cumulative return over the tracking period | Maximum drawdown | Initial gross exposure |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Original estimated tangency portfolio | **40.78%** | **−23.70%** | **5.389×** |
| Tangency portfolio using Ledoit–Wolf covariance shrinkage | 31.30% | −16.83% | 3.494× |
| Value-weighted portfolio | 11.33% | −13.09% | 1.000× |
| Equal-weighted portfolio of the same 30 stocks | 11.93% | −8.40% | 1.000× |

*Note: The tracking period runs from the opening on January 2, 2026, to the closing on October 6, 2026, rather than the full calendar year. Returns are cumulative, not annualized, and exclude full implementation costs. Initial gross exposure is the sum of the absolute long and short position values divided by net asset value. Sources: [2026 tracking results](../../research_2026/README.md) and [covariance-shrinkage comparison](../../research_2026/ledoit_wolf/README.md).*

The original tangency portfolio substantially outperformed the alternatives, showing that its advantage did not immediately disappear outside the estimation period. Its investment value therefore should not be dismissed outright. However, it also experienced a deeper drawdown, and the tracking exercise excludes financing costs, actual stock-borrowing conditions, trading costs, and forced liquidation caused by margin requirements. The portfolios are not matched for risk, so **a higher cumulative return does not establish superior risk-adjusted performance**. Moreover, although the weight estimates do not use 2026 returns, the stock-selection and methodological choices were made retrospectively in 2026. This is retrospective tracking outside the estimation sample, rather than a fully prospective out-of-sample test free of retrospective selection.

The Ledoit–Wolf comparison provides further support for a compromise allocation. Keeping estimated expected excess returns unchanged while applying covariance shrinkage reduces extreme positions and gross exposure, while retaining a relatively high return with a smaller drawdown in this tracking period. Nevertheless, part of the drawdown reduction may reflect the lower exposure, and the shrinkage portfolio still has gross exposure of **3.494 times** net asset value. The evidence therefore supports more robust, constrained optimization; it does not establish that the shrinkage portfolio is already the best allocation.

Third, Stages 2 and 3 do not provide sufficient evidence that abnormal returns will persist. The Stage 2 GRS test has a p-value of approximately **0.310**, so it does not reject the joint null that all CAPM alphas are zero at the 5% level. This neither establishes that every alpha is zero nor rules out individual stocks passing a separate significance screen. After adding size and value factors in Stage 3, MSFT's monthly alpha falls from **1.032% to 0.693%**, while WMT's falls from **1.008% to 0.737%**, with WMT's t-statistic declining to **1.56**. These changes show that some judgments about abnormal returns depend on model specification. However, absolute alpha declines for only 13 of the 30 stocks, so the three-factor model cannot be said to explain all abnormal returns. These tests do not directly assess the tangency strategy's future performance, but they caution against attributing its entire historical advantage to persistent investment skill. [Stage 3 results](https://github.com/shimarinshi/fe5108-portfolio-theory-lab/blob/main/artifacts/stage3/factor_interpretation.md)

Finally, recent market leadership supports both retaining exposure to large growth companies and controlling concentration. S&P Dow Jones Indices reports that the Magnificent Seven contributed approximately **55% of the S&P 500's total return over 2023–2025**. If AI-related earnings continue to materialize, large technology and growth companies may remain market leaders. [S&P market review](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/commentary/article/us-equities-market-attributes/) However, our value-weighted portfolio allocates only across the selected 30 stocks; it is not a comprehensive market index. Apple and Microsoft together account for approximately **49.68%**, rising to approximately **70.31%** when Meta and Tesla are included. Value weighting therefore also entails substantial concentration risk. [Portfolio weights](../../research_2026/ledoit_wolf/README.md)

Looking ahead, conditional scenarios are more appropriate than a single confident forecast. Large growth stocks may continue to lead if AI-related earnings keep expanding; a more diversified allocation may benefit if earnings improvements broaden across industries; and highly leveraged long–short strategies may become harder to implement if inflation or financing pressures increase. The IMF's April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report also identifies risks from market concentration, valuations, and the amplification of shocks through leverage. [IMF Global Financial Stability Report](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/gfsr/issues/2026/04/14/global-financial-stability-report-april-2026) These scenarios are not predictions validated by this project, so the entire allocation should not depend on the assumption that a single investment style will continue to dominate.

**Accordingly, we recommend using value weights as a benchmark, imposing concentration limits, and using weights from covariance-shrinkage optimization to guide limited active deviations.** Implementation should include caps on individual positions, gross exposure, and short selling. The size of the active allocation should then be determined through rolling out-of-sample evaluation that incorporates implementation costs. The current evidence supports this compromise, but does not establish an optimal blending proportion.
